History of Mars Exploration:

Throughout history, NASA has developed 20 missions aimed at investigating Mars. This ambitious exploration began with flybys and complex orbiters tasked with mapping the planet from space, paving the way for even more challenging missions—those seeking to reach the surface of the Red Planet.

Out of all those missions, exactly 9 successfully landed on the surface of Mars. From the first missions to achieve this, Viking 1 and Viking 2, to the innovative Mars Pathfinder, the unforgettable twin rovers Spirit and Opportunity, the Phoenix polar lander, and the InSight geophysical station, all the way to the advanced Curiosity and Perseverance, which remain active today collecting invaluable data.

However, those mechanical explorers that completed their lifespan did not return home; they have remained there, motionless in the silent Martian desert as true monuments to human curiosity. We invite you to discover the story of some of them.

Landing Sites on Mars:

This map shows the landing sites of NASA's Mars landers and rovers that have completed their missions or fallen silent: Viking 1 (1976–1982), Viking 2 (1976–1980), Mars Pathfinder and Sojourner (1997), Spirit (2004–2010), Opportunity (2004–2018), Phoenix (2008) and InSight (2018–2022).

NASA missions that are still operating, such as the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers, are intentionally not shown. This project focuses on spacecraft that were left behind on the surface: abandoned, but not forgotten.

Only NASA missions are included. Landers from other space agencies are outside the scope of this map.

The Vikings Missions, Pioneers on Mars

Decades ago, humanity took its most ambitious leap toward the Red Planet: the twin Viking 1 and Viking 2 missions. Designed as complete orbital and surface laboratories, these paired spacecraft didn't just visit Mars—they completely transformed our understanding of it.

High above, the massive orbiters mapped ninety-seven percent of the planet's surface, unveiling ancient dry riverbeds, colossal volcanoes, and global dust patterns for the very first time. On the ground below, the landers braved the freezing temperatures to capture the first high-definition panoramas of the Martian plains and analyze its crimson soil.

Working in perfect synchronization across space, the orbiters acted as both mapping powerhouses and vital communication links for the landers. Follow their historic double journey, and discover how this flagship fleet laid the foundation for every rover and orbiter exploring Mars today.

Sojourner: The first rover on Mars

On July 4th 1997, the Pathfinder Mission team renamed their lander on Mars the Carl Sagan Memorial Station to pay homage to the famous astronomer who had passed away the previous year. After an innovative landing protocol that included bouncing 15 times in a kilometer range, the lander could safely deploy a brand-new prototype on Martian ground. This wheeled device was supposed to capture images while it analyzed reddish soil and rocks. It was planned to last a week before shutting down, but this little hero was not ready to say goodbye so soon. Meet Sojourner, the first successful rover landed safely on Mars!

Work in Progress

Due to time constraints, we were not able to create all the animations we wanted. Some of the stories still pending are those of Spirit, Opportunity, Phoenix, InSight, and Ingenuity (which remain on the surface of Mars), and those of MAVEN, Mars Global Surveyor, and Mariner 9 (which were left in Martian orbit, although we currently cannot confirm whether they are still there).
